A product design exploration by Neha Bendale, Product Manager

01 | Why This Matters

The recommendation that dies in WhatsApp

Senior viewers in India have strong social circles. They talk about movies and shows constantly, over chai, on calls, in family groups. This is a bet on the simplest growth lever Netflix has: people trust their friends more than any algorithm.

What happens today

I watch a movie I love and share it with Yogesh on WhatsApp. Now the discovery of that recommendation depends entirely on him opening WhatsApp, finding my message among fifty others, and clicking through.

The journey is long and fragile: friend recommends, message sits on an external platform, user eventually finds it, clicks the link, Netflix opens, and only then does the watch begin.

The opportunity

What if Netflix surfaced that recommendation the moment Yogesh opens the app? A simple row on the home page: Recommended by Friends.

The journey collapses to: friend recommends, open Netflix, see the row, watch. Right now, a recommendation from a friend dies inside WhatsApp. The fix is not more chatter; it is bringing that trusted signal inside Netflix itself.

We are not building a social network. We are making the recommendations that already happen every day, effortless to act on.

The framing that shapes every decision in this deck
Core problem

Netflix is excellent at algorithmic recommendations, but viewers also depend on people they personally know. Those recommendations are scattered across WhatsApp, SMS, and phone calls, which means they get missed, require app switching, and force extra searching before a viewer can press play. Every one of those steps is lost conversion.

02 | Who We Are Building For

Designed for the 60 to 80 viewer

The primary segment is Indian Netflix users aged 60 to 80: moderate technology comfort, phones and tablets used mainly for WhatsApp, YouTube, and a handful of familiar apps. They prefer simple interfaces, lean on friends and family for what to watch, and often favour regional language or familiar content.

Persona

Jayant, 62 · The Quick Viewer

  • Watches a wide variety of content
  • Fast forwards the parts he finds boring
  • Wants to grasp the storyline quickly
  • Values recommendations that tell him what is worth his time

  • Watches thrillers, adventure, and romance
  • Genuinely enjoys discovering new kinds of content

My hypothesis: trusted recommendations beat algorithmic ones when it comes to actually pressing play. If sharing is easy and recommendations live inside Netflix, discovery gets faster and viewing follows.

The user hypothesis this design sets out to prove

04 | The Solution

Two surfaces, one small loop

The entire feature is a loop with two ends: a better way to share, and a place where shared titles land. Nothing else changes about Netflix.

A · Share with Friends

The existing Share action gets a new first-class option. Watching a title or viewing its details, the path is simply: Share → Share with Friends → pick a friend or group → send, with an optional personal note. The entire journey is three taps. If a viewer has to learn a new social workflow, we have already lost them.

NETFLIX Placement, next to the actions viewers already use
👍 Like 👎 Not for me 👥 Share with Friends

Share sits beside Like and Not for me, an interaction pattern viewers already know. One tap on Share with Friends, choose the group, done. Example message: Neha recommended The Intern · "You might enjoy this!"

B · The Recommended by Friends row

A new row on the Netflix home page holds every recommendation you have received, in the familiar content card format. Important principle: the row is contextual. It appears when you have at least one recommendation and stays out of the way when you do not.

05 | Design Considerations

Built for a TV remote, and for patience

1 · Primary viewing device is the TV

Senior viewers mostly watch Netflix on TV. On a remote, every extra menu is real friction, so the share flow cannot be Title → More Options → Share → Share with Friends → Select Group.

Instead the action lives on the title surface itself: 👍 | 👎 | 👥 Share with Friends. One click, select the group, done.

Design principle: minimise navigation steps, because each additional interaction on a remote multiplies effort.

2 · Low cognitive load, always
  • Large, clearly identifiable controls and selection targets
  • High readability and strong visual hierarchy
  • Minimal steps with simple, familiar terminology
  • Clear confirmation after every action
  • No unnecessary pop ups, menus, or choices
  • Consistent placement of the Share action

For senior users, simplicity is not a nice to have. It is a core product requirement.

The non negotiable design principle
06 | Privacy and Trust

Opt in, always. No exceptions.

Trust is the entire product. Break it once and the feature is dead, so social viewing is opt in at every step. Watching a title never automatically tells anyone anything.

Privacy principles
  • Sharing is always an explicit action
  • Watching never means sharing by default
  • The viewer chooses exactly which group receives it
  • Anyone can leave a group or mute recommendations
  • No public viewing activity, ever
Future opportunity, deliberately out

Once sharing behaviour is established, Netflix could explore "What your friends are watching". I am keeping it out of the MVP on purpose.

Recommended by Friends is an explicit action. What friends are watching is passive activity, and passive sharing carries far heavier privacy weight. It would only ever ship behind explicit consent: "Let your friends know you're watching this? Yes / No."

07 | Goals and MVP Scope

Ship the loop, nothing more

The user goal is faster, lower effort discovery through people they trust, without leaving Netflix. The business goal is more discovery, more starts, deeper engagement, and stickier repeat usage. The MVP is scoped to prove exactly one thing: the loop works.

In the MVP

  • Share with Friends flow
  • Friends and family groups
  • Recommended by Friends home row
  • Explicit privacy controls

Deliberately out

  • What friends are watching
  • Public profiles
  • Comments, likes, or a social feed
  • Automatic viewing status sharing
  • Any broader Netflix UI redesign
  • New content categories
08 | How We Measure It

One north star, honest guardrails

My primary metric is Recommendation to Watch conversion: of every recommendation received, how many lead to the recipient starting the title. Everything else tells us whether the loop is healthy, and whether we are earning trust or burning it.

Guardrails: the trust metrics

  • Recommendation mute rate and group abandonment: are we wearing people out?
  • Spam / report rate and privacy complaints: are we crossing a line?
  • Change in overall Netflix engagement: the feature must never hurt the core experience.
09 | Experiment and Definition of Success

Prove it, then scale it

Run a clean A/B test. The control is today's Netflix. The treatment adds Share with Friends plus the Recommended by Friends row. We then compare the full funnel: discovery → recommendation engagement → content start → watch behaviour.

The key question

Does adding trusted social recommendations help users find and start content more effectively?

Success means

The feature wins if trusted recommendations measurably reduce discovery friction and lift content starts, while privacy stays intact and the existing Netflix experience is untouched. If the guardrails move the wrong way, that is not success, whatever the conversion number says.

The core bet: Netflix does not need to become a social network. It needs to make trusted recommendations effortless to discover and act on.

The one line this entire deck bets on
